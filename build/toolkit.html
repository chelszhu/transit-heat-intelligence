Extreme heat and afternoon subway ridership: a station-level analysis

New York City subway · 428 station complexes · warm seasons (May–Sep) 2022–2024 · ≈190,000 station-day observations

Summary

1 · Data

The analysis combines high-frequency ridership, reanalysis weather, and built-environment data — all from public sources, so the workflow is reproducible by any agency with automated passenger counts, a weather feed, and basic station GIS.

2 · Empirical strategy

For each station complex i we estimate, by ordinary least squares, a log-linear model of daily afternoon ridership on felt temperature:

ln(Rit) = αi + βi·Ht + γi′Xt + εit,   Ht = max(0, apparent Tmax,t − 80°F)

Rit is afternoon boardings at station i on day t; Ht is a piecewise “hot-degrees” term that activates only above 80°F; and Xt collects day-of-week, calendar-month, year, U.S.-federal-holiday, and daily-precipitation controls. Because the outcome is logged, βi is a semi-elasticity — the approximate proportional change in afternoon ridership per additional °F of felt heat above 80°F. The heat penalty reported throughout is the fitted change at 95°F relative to an 80°F baseline, exp(15·βi) − 1. Standard errors are classical OLS (≈450 daily observations per station); a response is termed statistically significant when |t(βi)| ≥ 1.96 (two-sided, α = 0.05). We classify significant negative slopes as suppressed, significant positive as attracting, and the balance as neutral.

4 · Decomposing exposure: walk, wait, or platform

A statistically suppressed station tells us heat costs riders, but not where on the trip the heat is felt — and that is what determines who fixes it and how. We decompose each station's afternoon heat exposure into three components, scored 0–100 so they can be compared directly, and take the largest as the dominant burden.

The framing matters for a subway. A rider's heat load on the platform is a dose = intensity × duration: how hot the platform is, times how long they stand on it. We therefore split the on-platform experience into two distinct, separately-fixable components — platform (intensity) and wait (duration) — rather than treating them as one. (In an earlier bus-oriented version, "wait" meant curbside shelters and benches; on a subway the wait happens on the platform, so that definition simply collapses into "platform." Measuring the wait as exposure time keeps it a real, separate lever.) The three components:

  • Walk — approach heat. Scored from street-tree canopy density over the station's 5-minute network walkshed (the actual streets people use to reach the entrance): sparse canopy ⇒ a hot, unshaded approach. This is the one component on the public sidewalk, off MTA property.
  • Platform — heat intensity. How sun-exposed the platform structure is: elevated 100 (full sun, no relief), open-cut 70 (partly shaded by the trench), underground 55 (no direct sun but stagnant, radiant heat). This is a physical property of the structure, so it is scored on a fixed scale rather than ranked.
  • Wait — exposure duration. How long a rider is stuck on that platform, from scheduled service frequency: expected wait ≈ 30 ÷ (trains per hour), scored as each station's percentile across the system. Infrequent, single-line outer stations sit high; frequent trunk hubs sit low. Longer waits mean more time absorbing platform heat — the lever here is running more, more-reliable service (and cooling the unavoidable wait), not street furniture.

On the data. In this build the wait component is proxied from nominal peak trains-per-hour per line — a stand-in for MTA's GTFS scheduled headways and on-time-performance feeds, which a production deployment would read directly to capture real, time-of-day and reliability-adjusted waits. Because scores are percentiles (wait) or min–max-normalized (walk) across the system, the decomposition is a relative index: "wait-dominant" means the wait ranks worse here than at most stations, not an absolute number of minutes.

5 · From estimates to investment priority

To make the coefficients actionable, the tool's Future Heat Service Risk index scales each station's estimated penalty by afternoon ridership and by the projected annual frequency of extreme-heat days (NYC Panel on Climate Change, 4th assessment: heat-index days ≥95°F rise roughly six-fold, from ~6 to ~37 per year, by the 2050s), then weights the result by neighborhood social heat-vulnerability and by fixability. This converts an estimated slope into a fundable quantity — projected afternoon trips lost per year — and a red / amber / green priority tier used on the Dashboard tab.

6 · Robustness and limitations

  • Identification is associational, not causal. Day-of-week, month, year and holiday fixed effects plus a precipitation control absorb the leading seasonal and weather confounders, but unobserved shocks (special events, service changes, construction) are not differenced out.
  • Functional form. The 80°F threshold and log outcome impose a specific response shape; the sign and significance of results are stable under a linear-in-temperature specification and under a 78–82°F threshold.
  • Spatial temperature. Felt temperature is drawn from a single central-NYC point; borough-scale microclimate differences (waterfront cooling, urban-heat-island intensity) are not resolved.
  • A null is not “no effect.” A neutral coefficient is consistent with captive riders who endure the heat without changing behavior — a comfort and health cost a ridership regression cannot observe.
  • Attractors reflect destination demand (beaches, parks), not thermal comfort, and should be read as capacity signals rather than suppression.

7 · Intervention framework

Each diagnosis maps to a costed intervention, filtered by owner, delivery timeline and cost. The three levers are genuinely different in kind: street-tree shade on the approach lowers felt temperature by roughly 3°C but is slow and street-owned (Parks / DOT); shortening the wait with more frequent, more reliable service is an MTA operations-and-service-planning lever; and cutting platform heat with shade, reflective roofing or ventilation is MTA capital work. Because two of the three sit inside the MTA-operated station and only the approach is on the public street, the binding constraint is usually jurisdiction and timeline, not efficacy.

Decision framework

Every station follows the same transparent sequence: evidence → diagnosis → action → implementation filter → validation. The framework separates the observed signal from the intervention choice, then makes ownership, delivery and cost explicit.
